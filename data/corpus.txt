The ferry leaves the east bank at six in the morning and again every forty minutes until the light goes. Most days it carries the same people: the baker's two sons with their crates of bread, a nurse who works the night shift at the clinic across the water, a man who repairs radios and never seems to have one with him, and a rotating crowd of school children who are loud on the way over and quiet on the way back.

The river is wide here, wider than it looks from the hill road, and in the rainy months the current pushes the boat downstream so that the pilot has to aim well above the landing. He does this without looking at anything in particular. When people ask him how he knows the angle he says that the river tells him, which is not an answer, but it is the one he gives.

On the west bank there is a market that opens before the ferry arrives and closes an hour after noon. The stalls are arranged in rows by what they sell. Vegetables near the water, then grain and lentils, then cloth, then the tea stalls at the top where the old men sit and argue about the price of everything. A woman who sells onions keeps a notebook of every sale she has made for eleven years. She can tell you what onions cost on any day you name, and whether it rained.

The clinic is a low building painted pale green. It has four rooms, a generator, and a water tank on the roof that someone climbs up to check every evening. The nurse from the ferry has worked there for six years. She says the hardest part of the job is not the patients but the paperwork, and the second hardest part is the generator.

In the afternoon the town slows down. Shops pull their shutters halfway, dogs find shade under parked carts, and the only steady sound is the pump at the edge of the fields lifting water into the channels. The farmers time their work around that pump. When it stops, everyone knows within minutes, because the channels go quiet and someone has to walk out to see why.

Evenings belong to the riverbank. Families walk along the stone steps, vendors sell roasted corn and sweet tea, and the ferry makes its last crossings with its lamp lit. The pilot ties up at the east landing after the final trip, checks the ropes twice, and walks home along the hill road, where the river looks narrow again.

There is a small library above the post office. It holds about two thousand books, most of them donated, many of them missing a cover or the last few pages. The librarian has a habit of writing the missing endings herself on folded sheets of paper and tucking them into the back. Readers who notice are divided on whether this is a service or a crime. She keeps a separate shelf for books that are complete, and it is always the emptiest one.

Every year before the rains, the town repairs the embankment. It is not an official event. People simply arrive with baskets and shovels on the same weekend, as if they had agreed, and spend two days moving earth and stone. Children carry water. The tea stalls stay open late. Nobody is in charge, and yet the work gets done in roughly the same order every time, starting from the low section near the market and ending at the bend below the clinic.

The radio repairman has a theory that the town works like a radio. Each part does one small job and listens for the others. If one part goes quiet you can usually find it by listening to what is missing. He explains this to anyone who sits near him on the ferry. Most people nod politely. The nurse once asked him what part she was, and he said she was the fuse, because she stopped things from burning down. She laughed, and then thought about it for the rest of the crossing.

In the dry months the river shrinks and a sandbar appears in the middle, long and pale like the back of an animal. Children dare each other to swim out to it. The pilot steers around it with the same unbothered calm he uses for the floods. When asked which season is harder he says neither, because the river is only ever doing one thing at a time, and you only have to answer that one thing.
